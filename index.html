The Sick Economy: Why Reactive Healthcare Is Failing Us

The system we built

Picture the average person's relationship with healthcare. They feel fine, so they don't go. Then something happens — a diagnosis, a scare, an episode that lands them in a clinic or a ward. Treatment begins. Maybe it works. Maybe it becomes a management plan they carry for the rest of their life.

This isn't a failure of medicine. It's the design of it.

Most healthcare systems around the world are built around the crisis — the moment you become sick enough to need intervention. Hospitals, pharmaceutical pipelines, insurance structures, GP workflows: the overwhelming majority of healthcare resources flow toward people who are already unwell. Prevention, by comparison, gets a fraction of the attention and a fraction of the funding.

"Healthcare systems today are still largely reactive. We react, when it's too late."

— Dr. William Tan

Dr. William Tan has spent his career inside that system — first as a patient, then as one of its most accomplished practitioners. His perspective on what's broken, and what can fix it, carries a weight that pure statistics cannot.

A life that defied the odds — and what it taught him

William Tan was not yet three years old when he contracted polio. A vaccine existed. It was not compulsory at the time. Two tiny drops, he says, would have saved his legs.

Instead, he grew up in a wheelchair — and proceeded to use it to complete marathons across all seven continents, earn a medical degree, and become a practising neurosurgeon. His life is, by any measure, an extraordinary demonstration of what the human body and will can achieve under constraint.

But what Dr. William takes from his own story isn't triumph over adversity. It's a lesson about access, timing, and the difference a single preventive intervention can make.

"Just two tiny drops would have saved my legs. The vaccine existed — but it was not compulsory, then."

— Dr. William Tan

He isn't bitter about it. But he is motivated by it. Because the logic of "a small intervention now versus a large consequence later" is exactly the logic that preventive healthcare is built on — and exactly the logic that most people never apply to their own health until it's too late.

The gap nobody talks about

Singapore was recognised as a Blue Zone city in 2023 — one of the few places in the world where people demonstrably live longer than average. That's worth celebrating. But it also obscures a problem.

Around the world, and in Singapore specifically, people are living longer. But the gap between how long we live and how long we live well — what researchers call the healthspan–lifespan gap — remains stubbornly wide. On average, that gap is more than a decade.

What that means in practice: years of chronic disease management, declining mobility, escalating medical costs, and reduced quality of life in what should be the most experienced chapter of a person's existence. We are, collectively, surviving longer while not necessarily thriving longer.

The medicine that doesn't have a brand name

Dr. William's argument is not that modern medicine is bad. It's that we have systematically underinvested in the most powerful medicines available — ones that don't come in a blister pack, don't require a prescription, and don't make money for anyone.

There is a kind of "vaccine" suitable for everyone, that could save many of our lives in the future. They don't go by pharmaceutical names. They go by names like movement, sleep, nutrition, and social connection.

This is the evidence base for lifestyle medicine — the growing body of research showing that consistent, sustainable health behaviours can prevent, delay, or even reverse the progression of many chronic conditions. It is not alternative medicine. It is increasingly mainstream clinical science.

The challenge is structural. Lifestyle interventions are hard to monetise, hard to prescribe in a 15-minute consultation, and hard to measure in a quarterly earnings report. So despite the evidence, they remain under-prescribed and under-resourced in most healthcare settings.

What preventive care actually means

Preventive care is sometimes understood narrowly — annual check-ups, cancer screenings, vaccine schedules. These matter. But the deeper version of prevention is about understanding your own health data well enough to act before a condition develops, not after it's diagnosed.

That requires knowing your baseline. Your metabolic markers, your cardiovascular risk profile, your inflammatory state, your biological age relative to your chronological age. Most people don't have this picture. They find out something is wrong only when a symptom appears — by which point the underlying condition has often been developing for years.

Taking the bet

Dr. William ends the episode with a provocation rather than a prescription. His whole life, he says, has been about defying the odds. But he's not asking you to be exceptional. He's asking you to take the one bet that the evidence consistently supports.

"My whole life has been about defying the odds. But if there ever is a chance to make the odds work more in your favour — take it. Take a bet on your health."

— Dr. William Tan

The sick economy he describes isn't a conspiracy or a failure of good intentions. It's a system that evolved to respond to crisis, and that does so remarkably well. But it was never designed to prevent one. That gap — between what the system does and what our health actually needs — is where MORROW operates.

Prevention isn't a luxury. It's the most rational investment most of us will ever make.
